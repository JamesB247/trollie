# Trollie: Customer Value and Product Strategy Review

**Date:** 29 October 2026  
**Purpose:** Test the customer proposition against the founder's partner's feedback, existing South African shopping services, and relevant research.  
**Status:** Recommendations and testable hypotheses. Not an approved scope change, evidence of product-market fit, or a claim that proposed capabilities are already live.  
**Materials:** The supplied voice-note transcript; Trollie ShopriteX Partnership Proposal v3.0, dated 29 October 2026; the product decisions visible in this conversation.  
**Product preserved:** A purchasing and fulfilment service, including customer-controlled agent wallets and permissions. This review does not turn Trollie into a price-comparison-only website or remove the wallet.

## Executive conclusion

The partner's central objection is correct: the product is described more clearly from the perspective of its builder and its retail partner than from the perspective of the person expected to use and pay for it.

The existing proposal explains who may approve a purchase, how orders are controlled, and why retailers might allow agents to transact. It does not establish a compelling reason for an already-served household to change its shopping routine. This is not solved by replacing technical vocabulary with friendlier vocabulary. It requires selecting a household problem, delivering a better outcome, and measuring the difference against that household's existing alternatives.

**Recommended primary promise:** less work organising and completing the household shop, with a transparent delivered price and the customer's preferences preserved.

**Recommended reason to choose Trollie over one retailer's app:** one household plan and purchasing relationship across participating retailers, without rebuilding lists, preferences and instructions for each store. When a cross-store purchase genuinely saves money after all charges, show that saving. When it does not, say so and let the customer choose convenience, a single-store basket or their existing route.

**Recommended initial customer hypothesis:** the person organising a household's recurring shop who already combines retailers or spends substantial effort planning meals, keeping track of essentials, comparing baskets and resolving shopping problems. This is a role, not a gender or income stereotype.

**Do not lead with:** AI agents, protocols, wallets, payment rails, signed mandates, or a promise to sell everything from every shop. Those are mechanisms or unproven coverage claims.

**Do not assume uniqueness:** recipes, conversational shopping, remembered purchases and personalised suggestions are already part of the public propositions of major retailers. Woolworths' 8 October 2026 W.CHEF announcement is particularly relevant: a limited customer beta connects personalised meal ideas with ingredient purchasing through DASH. [S01-S05]

**The key product test:** does Trollie reduce the customer's total work, including onboarding, correcting the assistant, checking substitutions, receiving deliveries and chasing refunds? A system that transfers shopping work into supervising an assistant has not delivered the intended benefit.

The voice note is one substantial qualitative interview, not representative market validation. Its strength is the specificity of its shopping situations and objections. The following are paraphrases of the supplied transcript, not invented customer research. [U1]

| Remind me about preparation | Avoid remembering a second schedule | Optional preparation reminders linked to a selected recipe and confirmed meal date | Use reviewed instructions, not invented universal cooking or storage times |
| Save a meal from a photo or link | Turn inspiration into something usable later | A meal inspiration inbox, then a confirmed recipe and shopping draft | A plated-food image cannot establish every ingredient, allergen or quantity |
| Suggest ingredients I may already have | Avoid duplicate purchases | A quick 'already have / buy / not sure' review | Never require exhaustive pantry administration before first use |
| Find savings and preserve rewards | Improve actual household value | Whole-basket comparison with eligible loyalty, fees and delivery | A stored loyalty barcode does not establish reward eligibility through Trollie |

| Do not keep nagging about an abandoned cart | Preserve autonomy and attention | Quiet default; opt-in reminders; dismiss, snooze and mute controls | An abandoned draft is not a request for persuasion |
| Remember that I like an item | Avoid repeated explanation | Separate favourite, recurring draft and standing purchase instruction | Liking an item is not perpetual permission to spend |

### The central synthesis

She is asking for four outcomes: less mental work, fewer fragmented shopping tasks, better value on things she actually needs, and control without irritation. She is not asking for twenty independent AI features.

The first product response should therefore be a coherent weekly shopping journey. Adding every suggested feature simultaneously would risk producing exactly the complicated app she is objecting to.

## 2. Why the present proposal reads too technically

The proposal's cover leads with readiness for personal AI agents. Physical pages 4-5 then explain global agent and payment infrastructure. Page 6 introduces consent architecture and permission models. Page 8 emphasises synthetic tests. Page 9 contains the valuable statement about giving households time back, but most of that page returns to system roles and financial controls. Page 10 defines customers by voice preference and interest in agents. [U2, pp. 1, 4-10]

That order answers 'how can this be safely built?' before 'why would a household hire it?'

This is understandable in a partnership document: retailers do need assurance. However, even the retailer case needs evidence of an attractive customer outcome. Otherwise the proposal asks the retailer to invest effort in a safe channel that households may not need.

### Specific changes

| Current emphasis | Customer question left unanswered | Recommended change |
|---|---|---|
| Personal agents are coming | Why is my current shopping routine inadequate? | Open with a recognisable shopping problem and a completed outcome |
| Voice-first households | What important problem does voice solve here? | Treat voice and language as access choices within a purchasing journey |
| People who want AI agents | What recurring purchase will they delegate and pay for? | Define a separate power-user segment with a concrete task |
| Fixed quotes and consent | What will I pay, and how much effort remains? | Show one plain basket review, delivery count and fee before technical detail |
| Simulation test counts | Has anyone saved money or time using this? | Clearly distinguish engineering evidence from customer evidence |
| Retailer-brand mapping | Which household problem is being prioritised? | Start with customer missions; then show which merchant channels can fulfil them |
| Percentage fee or subscription | Why am I paying on top of an existing delivery subscription? | Show net value with all fees and the customer's existing entitlements |
| 'Everything' or 'whole shop' language | What happens when fresh food or a preferred shop is unsupported? | State the supported mission and visibly report missing coverage |

### Three documents, three purposes

Keep a short customer proposition and experience brief; a retailer partnership and economics document; and a detailed engineering/security specification. Do not weaken the latter. Stop asking it to act as consumer marketing.

The customer brief should be understandable before the words 'agent' or 'wallet' appear. The technical brief can remain rigorous about both.

## 3. Independent research: what it supports and what it does not

### 3.1 Shopping across stores is a real behaviour, not proof of demand for Trollie

PwC's South African 2025 consumer findings report that, among respondents concerned about food costs, 51% shop at different stores to maximise offers, 47% plan meals to limit impulse expenditure, and 45% use food already at home before buying more. These are the stated behaviours of a survey subgroup, not population-wide estimates or willingness to pay for an app. The methodology gives a global sample of 21,075 adults across 28 markets; it does not give the South African sample size on the methodology page reviewed. [S07, pp. 4, 16]

**Product implication:** test the combination of multi-store planning, meal-linked shopping and avoiding duplicates. Do not claim that deal-seeking makes every consumer a delivery or subscription prospect.

### 3.2 Affordability matters, but a national average is not a customer budget

Stats SA's 2022/23 Income and Expenditure Survey reports that food and non-alcoholic beverages accounted for 16.3% of aggregate household consumption expenditure. This is an observation-period statistic, not a 2026 price quote or a budget for a particular family. [S09]

**Product implication:** price the actual requested basket. Do not market an arbitrary amount as a complete month's groceries for every household.

### 3.3 Connectivity is not the same as purchasing readiness

The General Household Survey 2025 media release reports internet access of some kind for 85.6% of households. It does not establish reliable personal smartphone access, digital-payment readiness, courier coverage or willingness to delegate purchasing. [S08]

**Product implication:** keep the first experience lightweight and test real devices and connection quality. Do not use household connectivity as Trollie's addressable customer count.

### 3.4 The mental work is distinct from the physical shop

Daminger's 2019 research, based on interviews with 35 couples, distinguishes anticipating needs, identifying options, deciding and monitoring. That is a useful framework for the partner's feedback. It is not evidence that Trollie can automate all those activities or that the sample represents South Africa. [S10]

A separate study of 322 mothers found associations between cognitive household labour and wellbeing. Its self-reported, cross-sectional and comparatively advantaged sample limits causal and population conclusions. Trollie should not advertise a mental-health treatment benefit on this basis. [S11]

**Product implication:** measure remembering, planning, reviewing and follow-up effort, not just minutes spent tapping 'add to basket'. Include fathers, caregivers, shared households and solo shoppers rather than treating the workload as naturally female.

### 3.5 Convenience can have value without a lower cash total

Whillans and colleagues found associations between time-saving purchases and life satisfaction across several countries, and experimental evidence supporting time-saving purchases in a specific study. This supports investigating convenience value; it supplies neither a South African fee nor a Trollie-specific effect size. [S13]

**Product implication:** distinguish 'you spent less' from 'you paid for less work'. Both can be legitimate; presenting the second as the first is misleading.

### 3.6 Fewer interruptions is a design hypothesis worth testing

A randomised notification study with 237 participants found benefits from batching notifications compared with ordinary delivery, including reported attention and control. It was not a South African grocery study and does not establish the ideal Trollie frequency. [S12]

**Product implication:** test predictable, optional shopping digests and immediate necessary order updates. Do not import a three-times-daily research treatment as a marketing schedule.

### Overall evidence assessment

The evidence is sufficient to justify customer testing of the problem. It is not sufficient to claim nationwide demand, a particular saving, a subscription conversion rate, or a unique feature advantage.

## 4. What existing apps already offer

These are public first-party product descriptions checked for this review, not hands-on assessments of account-level availability, accuracy or service quality. Feature availability can vary by rollout, location and account. An announcement is not proof that every user can use a feature successfully today.

| Service | Relevant public proposition | Consequence for Trollie |
|---|---|---|
| Checkers Sixty60 / Pixie | Shoprite's launch material describes preference-based restocking and offers; ShopriteX subsequently describes conversational shopping and recipe-related use. The Sixty60 app lists shopping and delivery benefits. [S02-S04] | Remembering purchases, speaking to an assistant and suggesting meals cannot be presented as exclusive |
| Pick n Pay asap! / Penny | The retailer's app release notes describe lists, recipes, meal ideas and product suggestions, with text, voice and photo input into the basket. [S05] | Recipe-to-basket and multimodal entry are not standalone reasons to switch |
| Woolies DASH / W.CHEF | Woolworths announced the first customer beta on 8 October 2026, with dietary/preference-sensitive meal ideas and ingredient addition to DASH. Broader rollout is described as future work. [S01] | Do not claim Woolworths lacks a meal-planning assistant; distinguish beta from universal availability |
| Grocify | Its publisher describes whole-basket comparison, splitting with estimated driving cost, lists, alerts and loyalty-card storage. This is an adjacent comparison alternative, not established evidence of full purchasing and delivery execution. [S06] | Trollie's distinction must include completing purchases and handling outcomes, not just doing the comparison |
| A household's existing routine | Their normal retailer apps, saved lists, loyalty benefits and familiar support routes | The true baseline is what this person already uses, not a hypothetical shopper starting from nothing |

Sixty60's publisher lists Xtra Savings Plus at R99 monthly with delivery and personalised-offer benefits. Eligibility and exclusions require checking for the actual comparison. A household already paying for benefits should not be charged another subscription on the assumption that none exist. [S04]

### What remains potentially different

The proposed difference is the combination of a customer-owned shopping plan, retailer-independent preferences, eligible cross-store execution, clear delivered-cost choices, and one accountable support relationship.

This is a hypothesis about execution and alignment, not a declaration that no other business could do it. It becomes convincing only when Trollie can actually purchase from more than one relevant retailer and reliably complete the customer's task.

### Where Trollie should not insist on winning

A shopper who is happy with one retailer, its assistant and its delivery benefits may have little reason to switch. An urgent top-up can be better served by an established local delivery app. A person who likes choosing fresh produce in-store may not want delegation. Recognise these cases rather than declaring every household a prospect.

A narrow successful entry point is better than a broad claim of superiority that cannot survive the first checkout comparison.

## 5. Recommended positioning and the incentive to use Trollie

### Positioning to test

**Trollie helps you organise and complete your household shop across participating stores, remembering your choices and showing the full delivered cost before you commit.**

The primary emotional benefit is relief from recurring shopping administration. The functional proof is a correctly completed basket. Financial benefit is demonstrated when the comparable delivered total is lower, not assumed merely because some shelf prices are lower.

### Three value pillars

**Less to remember:** regular items, confirmed preferences, selected meals and a shopping rhythm.

**A sensible total:** the whole basket with delivery, service charges and actually usable benefits. Trade-offs are visible.

**The task gets finished:** orders, substitutions, delivery status, missing items and refunds are managed through one service, without removing consumer choice or retailer identity.

### Why this is not a pivot to comparison

The comparison is a decision inside a service that buys. The outcome is not a list of links telling the customer to finish the job in several other apps. When retailer access is unavailable, say that and offer a clearly labelled handoff rather than calling it a completed Trollie purchase.

### Savings versus convenience

Do not make the customer choose an ideology. Let them state priorities for a particular shop: lowest eligible delivered cost, fewer deliveries, a specific deadline, or strict brands. The founder still needs a primary customer and marketing promise; the product can support more than one outcome without pretending they are always simultaneously maximised.

### A useful switching test

Before asking someone to move their regular shop, answer:

1. What do you do for this household that their current apps do not do well enough?
2. What will it cost after their existing delivery and loyalty benefits?
3. What work must they still do, including supervising Trollie?
4. What happens when a favourite item is unavailable or a parcel is missing?
5. Can they try the service without moving all their shopping or funding a large balance?

If those answers are weak, an AI demonstration is not a sufficient reason to change behaviour.

## 6. Who it is for: the requested customer perspectives

These are proposed research segments, not observed market-size estimates. A person may occupy several of them. Recruit by the shopping job, not by assuming all mothers, women, fitness customers or technical users behave alike.

### 6.1 The household organiser: parent, partner or caregiver

**Job:** 'Make sure the household has what it needs without me running a second project every week.'

**Example:** Keep the usual lunchbox items, include ingredients for two selected dinners, retain a particular brand, omit things still in the cupboard and deliver in a window that works around family commitments.

**Reason to use Trollie:** fewer repeated decisions and fewer missed ingredients, across the stores the household actually uses.

**What the experience needs:** a shared draft list; named contributors; a designated payer; a few reusable preferences; a review of what changed since the last shop; optional meal and preparation reminders. Shared-list contribution need not confer permission to spend.

**What would make it worse:** having to maintain exact stock counts, answer ten separate substitution questions, accept three delivery visits, or reconcile which family member added what.

**Proof to collect:** active planning and correction time, missing essentials, unwanted duplicates, delivery interruptions, actual household expenditure and repeat use without incentives.

**Priority:** strongest initial candidate when the household already has recurring multi-store friction. Not automatically every parent.

### 6.2 The fitness or nutrition-focused customer

**Job:** 'Make my existing meal or nutrition plan practical to buy and repeat within my budget.'

**Example:** Import a user-supplied meal plan, scale approved servings, compare eligible product labels and assemble the groceries for several lunches without changing their essential constraints.

**Reason to use Trollie:** execution of a chosen plan and better visibility into product trade-offs, rather than generic motivational advice.

**What the experience needs:** verified brand/variant matching; traceable nutrition data; clear units; distinction between per-100-g, per-serving and whole-pack values; explicit acceptance of changes; cost and taste preferences alongside any nutrition target.

**What not to do:** infer that weight loss is the goal, replace all dairy with fat-free products, prescribe medical diets, or calculate an exact nutritional profile from a meal photograph. WHO describes healthy diets as depending on individual needs and context, not a universal single-product rule. [S14]

**Influencer-specific job:** save and share a clearly attributed meal and shopping template. That may be a distribution channel, but an audience does not prove the creator is a profitable recurring customer. Keep any paid recommendation visibly separate from a cheapest-basket result.

**Priority:** a useful secondary research cohort. A credible food-data capability is a dependency, not just a prompt.

### 6.3 The convenience customer who wants the shop handled

**Job:** 'I am busy; please get my usual things without turning it into a conversation.'

**Example:** Repeat the confirmed household shop with a few changes, preserve locked favourites, select the preferred delivery window and show only exceptions needing a decision.

**Reason to use Trollie:** low effort and reliable follow-through. This person may prefer one delivery over saving a small amount through splitting.

**What the experience needs:** one-tap usual shop, optional text or voice, no forced meal planning, transparent fee and responsive exception handling. The service should be useful even when the customer ignores every advanced feature.

**What would make it worse:** conversational onboarding that takes longer than a retailer's reorder button, or compulsory daily engagement.

**Priority:** plausible commercial cohort, but compare against existing repeat-order tools rather than assuming they are inadequate.

### 6.4 The budget-focused shopper

**Job:** 'Help me buy the intended list for less overall, not just find a discounted item.'

**Example:** Keep exact quantities and essential brands; show whether a second store still saves after another delivery charge; suggest a future bundle rather than purchasing an isolated special.

**Reason to use Trollie:** verified net value and fewer trips or comparison tasks.

**What the experience needs:** reproducible same-basket comparisons, current eligibility, fee visibility, affordable trial, supported collection where available, and a strict approved budget.

**What would make it worse:** a percentage fee that absorbs the saving, adding unnecessary products to qualify for delivery, or showing discounts the customer cannot actually redeem.

**Priority:** important research segment but potentially difficult paid-acquisition and delivery economics. Social need is not automatically proof of a viable commercial fee.

### 6.5 The technical user with their own agent

**Job:** 'Let my assistant complete an authorised South African shopping task without giving it unrestricted financial power.'

**Example:** A chosen agent requests a basket, sees the permitted result and executes only through a customer-approved purchase or bounded standing instruction.

**Reason to use Trollie:** local merchant access and trustworthy execution, rather than another chatbot.

**What the experience needs:** a documented connector, clear scopes, agent allocations, reliable status, revocation and a simple human approval surface. This can be a dedicated advanced onboarding path.

**What would make it worse:** a connector that can recommend but cannot order, a simulated-only merchant network presented as live, or repeated approval of harmless actions without a useful purchase result.

**Priority:** preserve the capability and test it separately. Technical enthusiasm must not be used as evidence of ordinary household demand.

### Recommended first customer definition

Start with digitally comfortable household organisers in the actual pilot delivery area who already shop from multiple stores or have a recurring, observable planning burden. Include a smaller comparison cohort of happy single-retailer customers and power users.

Do not lock the market definition to Fourways, a specific income band, mothers, English speakers or gym users without local evidence. Access to merchants and an operational delivery route also constrain who can receive the promised outcome.

## 7. The first experience should demonstrate the benefit, not explain the machinery

### A proposed ordinary-household journey

**Entry:** 'Send your list, repeat your usual shop, or choose a meal.' Do not begin by asking the person to learn what an agent is.

**First useful input:** a short list, a customer-uploaded receipt or a few favourite items. Ask only for missing information that affects the current task. Get delivery area and timing early enough to avoid an attractive but unserviceable basket.

**A small preference check:** identify the items that cannot change. Let the customer say 'same brand', 'any equivalent' or 'ask me'. Do not require completion of a long household profile.

**A useful proposal:** show a recommended complete option, a clear alternative when worthwhile, the actual stores, delivery visits, fees and missing items. A recurring draft should highlight changes rather than make the person inspect every unchanged line from scratch; the full basket must still be accessible before approval.

**The decision:** let the customer trade a stated cash difference against a stated delivery difference. For example, two deliveries may be cheaper but less convenient. Neither should be hidden behind the word 'best'.

**Execution:** Trollie places supported orders and supplies one customer status view. It should manage exceptions rather than forward a sequence of unexplained merchant messages.

**Afterwards:** ask whether the preferences or next-shop suggestions were useful. Save only the information the customer chooses to retain. A missed or incorrect item remains part of the evaluation, even when support rescues the order.

### The desired moment of value

The customer should reach a moment such as: 'It remembered the products I care about, included what I needed for the meal, explained whether the extra delivery was worthwhile, and then actually sorted the order.'

That is more persuasive than a tour of the wallet, agent settings or security controls. It must be demonstrated, not claimed as an achieved result.

### Do not require a chat for every action

Some tasks are easier with buttons and lists: 'Repeat last order', 'I still have this', 'Skip this week', 'Keep this brand', 'One delivery only', 'Stop reminders'. Conversation should reduce typing and clarification work, not introduce conversational friction to replace a familiar button.

### Suggested home screen priorities

1. The next planned shop or a simple list-entry action.
2. A short set of items to review, with reasons.
3. A relevant meal or deal only when it helps that shop.
4. Current orders and any action genuinely needed.

Place full history, budgets, wallet funding and connected agents in clear secondary navigation. Do not hide important balances or fees, but do not make financial setup the welcome experience for every visitor.

## 8. Savings: calculate the whole result, not the attractive headline

### A reproducible comparison

Compare the customer's existing feasible way of buying the same approved items with Trollie's feasible way, at a comparable time and destination.

Include item prices, quantities, delivery, service fees, required checkout charges and immediately usable discounts. Treat tips consistently. Show any new subscription charge separately and assess it over an honest period of use. Do not count the full monthly cost of an existing subscription as an avoided expense unless the customer would actually cancel it.

Conditional future cashback is not cash saved at this checkout. List it separately with conditions and uncertainty. Do not subtract it twice if it has already affected the quoted price.

A different brand, pack size, omitted ingredient, unavailable product or later arrival can make two baskets non-equivalent. Show the trade-off rather than converting it into a false percentage saving.

### Synthetic example: all amounts are invented

The table assumes identical intended goods, and a baseline customer whose qualifying direct order has no additional delivery fee because of benefits they already hold. It is not a Checkers, Woolworths, courier or Trollie rate card.

| Route | Goods | Additional delivery | Trollie service fee | Total payable | Difference from usual route |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Customer's usual eligible direct order | R1,000 | R0 | R0 | R1,000 | Baseline |
| Trollie, same merchandise price | R1,000 | R0 | R25 | R1,025 | R25 more |
| Trollie, modest two-store merchandise saving | R900 | R70 | R25 | R995 | R5 less |
| Trollie, stronger two-store merchandise saving | R850 | R70 | R25 | R945 | R55 less |

The R100 merchandise saving in the third row becomes a R5 delivered saving. The first Trollie route might still be attractive to a convenience customer, but it is not a money-saving route.

### Small specials and batching

An item R2 cheaper at a second store is not necessarily worth a second delivery. Conversely, a low-income shopper may genuinely care about R2 when no new trip or fee is involved. Do not dismiss the amount; evaluate the total consequences.

Offer 'include with my next shop', 'collect with my existing trip' where supported, or 'buy now at this total'. Do not manipulate the customer into buying unneeded products to meet a minimum. Sometimes the best recommendation is not to order.

### Quantity, unit price and storage

A larger pack may cost less per unit but require more cash now, occupy storage or go unused. Keep requested quantity separate from a bulk-buy recommendation. Show cash outlay and any accepted quantity change. Do not buy more merely because the unit price is lower.

### Pricing creates a product incentive

The proposal allows a percentage-of-basket service fee and a subscription. [U2, pp. 14, 16] A percentage fee can make the service feel expensive on larger shops and can sit awkwardly beside a promise to reduce household spending. That does not make it inherently wrong, but the conflict must be tested and controlled.

Compare a transparent fixed task/order fee, a capped percentage fee, and an optional subscription for demonstrably frequent use. Research should reveal the sustainable price; this report does not establish one. A low sticker price with unreliable support is not a successful offer.

Keep commission from influencing an answer labelled 'lowest delivered cost'. Distinguish paid placement from the recommendation engine. Document who funds rewards; a hoped-for retailer commission is not a funded loyalty programme.

### Cash, effort and avoided waste are separate ledgers of value

Show realised cash savings from completed purchases. Report user-measured effort reduction separately. Treat avoided waste and avoided duplicate purchases as hypotheses unless the household confirms them. Do not inflate savings by assigning a wage to every minute saved or assuming every unpurchased item would have been wasted.

## 9. What to build and validate first

A product should not be assembled by treating every desirable feature as an immediate requirement. The following order preserves the end-to-end purchasing proposition.

| Priority | Capability | Reason | Release condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core | A complete supported basket that can actually be bought and delivered | No value proposition survives repeated incomplete orders | Merchant, payment and delivery readiness |
| Core | Total delivered cost and visible trade-offs | Answers the switching and savings objection | Comparable, fresh, eligible inputs |
| Core | Confirmed favourites, reusable lists and quick repeat | Reduces effort immediately without waiting for a model to learn | Customer control and accurate item matching |
| Core | Clear issue handling in one place | Finishes the task after checkout | Support ownership and accurate remedy status |
| Early | A next-shop list and simple replenishment suggestions | Addresses remembering without requiring full pantry tracking | Confidence and correction path |
| Early | A small set of saved meals and ingredients | Tests the partner's meal-shopping job | Recipe provenance and actual ingredient coverage |
| Early | Optional quiet digest and shopping-time reminders | Fits the household routine | User controls and notification measurement |
| Conditional | Goal-specific product comparisons | Valuable for fitness and other intentional shoppers | Reliable labels, approved constraints and safe boundaries |
| Conditional | Fresh-food meal fulfilment | Necessary for many Sunday-dinner and fitness use cases | Category-suitable picking, handling and delivery |
| Retained specialist path | Customer wallets, agent allocations and standing instructions | Enables budgeting and delegated buying | Synthetic proof first; appropriate live operating arrangement |
| Retained specialist path | Connect-your-own-agent | Serves power users without dominating ordinary UX | Explicit connector and financial-authority boundaries |

This is a proposed customer-priority map, not permission to weaken existing payment controls or to delete the wallet work already requested by the founder.

## 10. Two important mismatches with the current pilot

### 10.1 Ambient goods cannot validate the whole meal promise

The proposal's live pilot starts with ambient packaged goods. [U2, p. 13] The partner's strongest stories involve milk, cheese, lamb, fresh meal ingredients and a complete household shop. Those require capabilities beyond the stated pilot category.

A pantry pilot can test recurring lists, comparing supported staples, useful reminders, payment trust and support. It cannot demonstrate that Trollie removes the household's whole grocery burden if the household must separately buy everything fresh.

Choose explicitly between:

- A narrowly described pantry-restocking trial, with success measured on that mission; or
- A later, operationally ready mixed-basket trial including the fresh categories necessary for the selected meal scenarios.

Do not make fresh-food delivery live merely to improve the marketing story. But do not use a successful dry-goods order as evidence that the Sunday-meal promise is solved.

### 10.2 One retail group cannot validate independent cross-chain purchasing

The current proposal is for a Shoprite Group pilot. [U2, pp. 3, 13] That can prove a useful delegated-shopping journey within agreed Group channels. It does not prove Trollie's ability to buy a combination of Woolworths favourites and another retailer's cheaper staples.

Preserve the longer-term retailer-independent vision, but label pilot coverage truthfully. A second permissioned retailer and actual fulfilment evidence are required before advertising cross-chain execution. The partner's examples of stores in the same mall are customer stories, not verified merchant access or courier terms.

These distinctions matter because otherwise a prospective customer hears the future product while the team measures a materially different present product.

## 11. Personalisation that does not become another chore

### 11.1 Replenishment should express uncertainty

Use three understandable states: 'you confirmed you still have it', 'you may be running low', and 'we do not know'. Purchasing thirty days ago is not proof of consumption: household size, travel, guests, spoilage and purchases elsewhere can change the pattern.

Start with customer-confirmed cadence. A simple editable schedule may outperform an opaque forecast in the first few orders. Record purchase and delivery outcomes before attempting sophisticated consumption inference.

Give useful corrections: still have plenty, bought elsewhere, not buying this anymore, remind me later. A single correction should improve the next suggestion without forcing the user to maintain a full inventory.

### 11.2 Preference is not purchase authority

Separate:

- 'I like this': remember a preference.
- 'Suggest this next shop': place it in a reviewable draft.
- 'Include this every fortnight': create a recurring draft with clear conditions.
- 'Buy this under these limits': a separately approved standing instruction.

Do not quietly promote one into another. This directly addresses the partner's desire for favourites that can be removed and the founder's desire for genuinely delegated purchasing.

### 11.3 Taste and household diversity

Remember ordinary likes and dislikes without making identity-based assumptions. A family can have conflicting preferences. Shared lists need conflict resolution and an accountable purchaser, not a model deciding whose preferences matter most.

Do not apply one adult's fitness target to children's meals or to the entire household. Let the shopper choose whose plan a change serves.

### 11.4 Nutrition comparisons require clear claims

Replace 'healthier cheese' with an explicit requested comparison such as lower sodium, different saturated-fat content, a chosen protein target or lactose-related preference, supported by product data. Let the person choose based on price and taste too.

A general lifestyle intention needs clarification; a clinical condition needs an appropriate professional plan rather than automated medical advice. Missing allergen information is not proof of allergen absence. A customer-selected product restriction must not be traded away for a lower price. [S14]

### 11.5 Meal inspiration versus a verified recipe

A photo can suggest a dish; it does not establish the original recipe. Ask the person to confirm the proposed version and portions. Preserve a supplied recipe's source and do not present an invented reconstruction as the exact original.

The data needed for a useful meal-to-basket flow includes servings, ingredient quantities, product packs, pantry assumptions, acceptable substitutions, cooking date and shopping lead time. Combine duplicate ingredients across selected meals before quoting, but ask before changing pack quantities beyond what was authorised.

Recipe prompts and pantry suggestions should make preparation easier without guaranteeing exact nutrients or a clinically appropriate diet.

### 11.6 Preparation reminders

The partner's reminder to prepare a Sunday meal is a good example of timely assistance. Link it to an actual chosen recipe and confirmed timing, not a generic message that every lamb dish needs twelve hours of marination.

Use vetted food-handling information. For example, FDA guidance says food should remain refrigerated while marinating. [S15] A helpful reminder must not confidently invent unsafe preparation or storage instructions.

## 12. A notification policy that respects the customer

The partner's objection to abandoned-cart urgency is an explicit product requirement to consider, not a minor marketing preference. [U1]

### Suggested classes

| Message class | Proposed treatment |
|---|---|
| Delivery arriving, payment problem or a genuine order exception | Timely, concise and actionable; distinguish factual status from promotion |
| A decision the customer must make for an active order | Explain the deadline and what happens without a response; never treat silence as fresh purchase consent |
| A scheduled shop the customer requested | One predictable reminder or digest at the selected time |
| A likely replenishment need | Include in the next digest unless the customer requests a specific alert |
| A special on a saved product | Opt-in, relevant to the planned basket and net value; explain expiry only when verified |
| Abandoned draft or unused basket | No default pressure sequence; offer a reminder only at the customer's request |
| Seasonal inspiration | Optional, easy to mute and subordinate to the actual shopping mission |

Start by testing one chosen shopping digest rather than daily alerts. That is a design hypothesis, not a research-backed universal frequency. Provide quiet hours, snooze, dismiss and channel controls. Avoid leaking sensitive purchases or dietary details in lock-screen text.

Do not optimise solely for open rate. Track whether messages led to a useful completed task, whether customers muted them, whether they felt interrupted and whether prompted purchases were regretted or cancelled.

The successful service may reduce sessions and notifications. More screen time is not necessarily customer success for a product promising to take shopping off someone's mind.

## 13. Wallets and agents remain in the product

The founder has explicitly restored wallets and agent allocations to the intended product. This review does not reverse that decision. [U3]

For ordinary customers, describe the benefit as a shopping budget or money set aside for approved purchases. Explain available money, reserved money, completed spending and refunds in ordinary language. For the technical user, expose the agent-specific controls and history in the advanced path.

Make a first useful quote possible before a large top-up or complex account setup. Where the chosen live model allows both, offer pay-per-order and optional pre-funding; the final operating arrangement still controls what can be enabled. Do not promise real stored-value availability from a synthetic demonstration.

Funding a wallet is not the same as authorising an agent to buy anything. A person who has not supplied a standing instruction should still control individual purchases. A person who has supplied a narrowly bounded instruction should not have to confirm every harmless planning action.

A wallet is not a unique customer benefit merely because it exists. Its value must show up as better spending control, easier authorised delegation or less repeated checkout effort. Do not require all customers to value it for the same reason.

## 14. A customer-first business model

### The tension to manage

A retailer may value larger baskets and more frequent purchases. A household may value spending less, buying only what is needed and receiving fewer deliveries. Trollie can earn revenue while serving the household, but its recommendation rules must make that alignment credible.

### Proposed principles

- Publish the service fee before approval, not after the useful work is done.
- Label sponsored placement; it cannot covertly determine a cheapest-basket answer.
- Include all material charges in the choice presented to the customer.
- Preserve usable loyalty benefits where supported, and disclose where they are not.
- Recommend waiting, combining or not purchasing when that is the best match for the customer's instructions.
- Do not turn temporary promotional credits into claimed recurring savings.
- Avoid promising lower-income fee waivers funded by a commission that is still under negotiation.
- Price support, failed attempts, returns and delivery exceptions into the business analysis.

The proposal's future Xtra Savings linking is explicitly not part of the minimum pilot. [U2, pp. 3, 12] Therefore a comparison that assumes those benefits already transfer would be invalid. Broader loyalty programmes require the same product-by-product check.

### Subscription test

Test payment after demonstrating a real benefit. For repeat shoppers, compare the actual monthly total and effort with their existing subscriptions. A small subscription is not cheap if the service is rarely useful, and a larger fee is not necessarily unattractive when it removes substantial work reliably.

Do not rely only on asking 'would you pay?'. Observe a real choice with a stated, legitimate fee once the live trial is properly authorised. Refusal, preference for the usual app and non-use are valid results.

## 15. A proposed validation programme

This is a research plan, not completed research, not a promise of future background work, and not a substitute for existing live-money and merchant gates.

### Stage 1: problem interviews and observed current shopping

Recruit approximately 24 people for formative work: eight household organisers, six fitness/meal-plan shoppers, six convenience shoppers and four personal-agent users. Segment overlap is expected; assign a primary recruitment category and record the other characteristics.

Include people who do not like the idea, people loyal to a single retailer, different supported languages, shared and low-end phones, several budget levels and people with existing delivery subscriptions. Do not recruit only the founder's friends or technical enthusiasts. The sample is not nationally representative.

Ask participants to bring a recent list, receipt or order and walk through the last actual shop. Obtain consent and minimise personal data in research records.

Useful questions:

1. What triggered the last shop, and who remembered what was needed?
2. Which stores or apps did you use, and why those?
3. Which benefits, preferred brands or delivery windows would you refuse to lose?
4. What was missing, duplicated, substituted or bought unnecessarily?
5. How much active time went into planning, ordering and following up?
6. What shopping work do you actually enjoy and want to keep?
7. When would an extra delivery be worth the saving?
8. Which notifications were helpful, ignored or muted?
9. What would make you refuse to trust a service with the order?
10. After seeing a realistic quote including fees, which route would you choose and why?

Do not use a feature wish list as the main interview. Ask about behaviour before presenting the solution.

### Stage 2: concept and comprehension test

Compare three short messages in counterbalanced order: less shopping administration; lower eligible delivered cost; and complete a selected meal or recurring shop. Do not feature 'AI' in every version and thereby prevent testing whether the outcome matters independently.

Ask participants to explain what they expect the service to do, what they think they still need to do and what would make them choose their usual app instead. Record misunderstanding, particularly around automatic spending, fresh-food coverage and one versus several deliveries.

Test simple prototype tasks: repeat a shop, change one preference, see the actual fee, refuse an unwanted substitution, defer an item, stop reminders and find help for a missing parcel. Measure whether assistance was needed.

### Stage 3: matched-basket evidence

For the same real list, location and comparable quote time, compare the customer's normal feasible checkout with Trollie's feasible option. Use confirmed brands, quantities, eligibility, delivery windows and fees. Retain negative and zero-saving cases.

Do not send customers through duplicate real purchases merely to create a comparison. Use quote comparisons for cost, and properly authorised normal shopping attempts for operational evidence. Time-based comparisons across different weeks must acknowledge changes in lists, promotions and household circumstances.

### Stage 4: an assisted longitudinal trial

After commercial and legal gates, invite a smaller group to use the service through at least two relevant shopping cycles. Separate any artificial introductory discount from normal pricing. Record all human assistance, including founder labour.

A weekly shopper and a monthly shopper need different observation periods. 'No second order in thirty days' does not mean the same thing for both. Conversely, a free reminder opened twice is not a repeat purchase.

Use the participant's real alternative as the control condition. A sophisticated Pixie, Penny or W.CHEF user should be allowed to use the capabilities available to them. Do not compare Trollie against a deliberately impoverished version of a competing app.

### What to measure

| Question | Measure |
|---|---|
| Did the service complete the intended task? | Whole intended-list coverage; accepted and fulfilled quantities; mandatory preferences preserved |
| Did it reduce work? | Active planning, corrections, approvals, delivery interruptions and support time; perceived effort separately |
| Did it save cash? | Realised matched delivered-cost difference including Trollie fees and usable benefits |
| Did it preserve control? | Unwanted substitutions, unexpected purchases, ability to stop or edit; any unauthorised spend |
| Did reminders help? | Useful actions, snoozes, opt-outs and reported irritation, not just open rates |
| Did replenishment help? | Accepted suggestions, false positives, missed needs and correction burden |
| Did meals become practical? | Ingredients sourced, portions correct, pantry assumptions corrected and planned meal actually feasible |
| Would users choose it again? | Repeat at the normal stated price on the next relevant shopping occasion |
| Can the business afford the service? | Contribution after real variable costs, failed attempts and human interventions |
| Who is underserved? | Results by primary shopping job, current benefits, device, language and coverage |

### Proposed decision criteria, not industry benchmarks

Before recruitment, write down the minimum useful benefit and affordable operating cost for each cohort. Suggested starting questions are whether a household values a noticeable reduction in active work, whether a savings-led order remains cheaper after all charges, and whether the person chooses Trollie again without pressure.

Do not label a five-rand saving with two extra deliveries a success unless the participant values that trade-off. Do not label a more expensive convenience order a failure if the participant knowingly prefers it and the service earns a sustainable margin.

An unauthorised purchase, serious preference violation or unsafe handling is not offset by a good average score. Pause the relevant operation, resolve the issue and retain it in the results.

### What would weaken the business thesis

Repeated correction effort exceeds the work saved; most customers prefer their incumbent benefits after seeing Trollie fees; the apparent savings depend on unavailable rewards; only subsidised users return; unsupported fresh items force a second full shop; or every successful order requires unpriced manual intervention.

Those results call for changing the scope, pricing or target segment, not adding more AI features to disguise the problem.

## 17. The decisions to make next

**First:** choose the first customer job. My recommendation is the recurring household shop with observable planning or multi-store friction, rather than 'anyone who likes AI'.

**Second:** decide what the pilot can honestly fulfil. Pantry-only is a legitimate first mission; a whole-meal promise needs the right fresh-food route.

**Third:** build one customer-facing journey around that job. Use existing commerce and wallet work underneath it. Do not restart the platform merely to improve positioning.

**Fourth:** run the proposed competitor task comparison and real-list research. The partner's feedback is a strong starting point, not a substitute for contrasting households.

**Fifth:** choose the fee, reminder rhythm and split threshold from observed customer choices and actual costs. Keep negative results visible.

**Sixth:** rewrite the opening of the proposal around the evidence. Move global agent developments and engineering assurance behind the customer story, not out of the document.

### Final recommendation

Keep the ambition: household purchasing across stores, with personal preferences, customer control and agent-enabled execution.

Change the order of explanation and proof. Lead with what the household no longer has to remember, compare or chase. Show the actual delivered value. Demonstrate that the shopping gets finished. Then explain the safe technology and optional wallet that make it possible.

The partner's feedback is not a request for a less capable product. It is a request for a product whose capabilities clearly improve ordinary life.
